Highlight of the day

“Irradiance describes the sun’s momentary power; irradiation describes the energy collected over time.”

Key discussion: Solar Resource Fundamentals

What I learned

Irradiance is the amount of solar power arriving at a surface at a particular moment. It is measured in W/m². Irradiation, or insolation, is the accumulated solar energy over time, commonly measured in kWh/m².

When modelling a PV module, irradiance is used for momentary output predictions, while irradiation is used to estimate daily energy yield.

GHI, DNI & DHI

GHI — Global Horizontal Irradiance — is the total solar irradiance received on a horizontal surface. It includes both direct and diffuse radiation and is commonly used for flat-plate solar systems.

DNI — Direct Normal Irradiance — measures direct sunlight on a surface perpendicular to the sun’s rays. It is especially important for concentrating solar systems.

DHI — Diffuse Horizontal Irradiance — is sunlight scattered by the atmosphere, including air molecules, dust, water vapour, and pollutants, that reaches a horizontal surface.

Measurement & modelling

A pyranometer is a scientific sensor used to measure solar irradiance. It captures both direct sunlight and diffuse sky radiation.

Common types of pyranometers include thermopile and silicon photocell sensors. A detector beneath a glass dome converts sunlight into a small electrical signal proportional to its intensity.

The zenith angle, θz, is the angle between the sun and the vertical direction directly above an observer.

A sun-position diagram or simulation can track how the sun moves across the sky and show how panel tilt and orientation affect plane-of-array irradiance.

Peak sun hours

Peak Sun Hours represent the total solar energy received at a location during a day. They compress a day of changing irradiance into an equivalent number of hours at 1,000 W/m².

A location may have 12 hours of sunlight but only 5.5 peak sun hours because solar intensity changes throughout the day. PSH is useful for solar-system sizing and expected-energy calculations.

Application idea

Build an AI-assisted tracker that records daily sun hours and sky conditions, then predicts the following day. The project could also compare locations by maximum and expected solar energy using simulated sun position, GHI, DNI, DHI, panel orientation, and weather conditions.

Highlight of the day

“A transistor can switch or amplify — but underneath both jobs, it's just a gate controlling a much bigger flow.”

From Notes on Electronics

My note

A transistor is a semiconductor device that acts as a switch or an amplifier, boosting a small signal into a larger one. As a switch, a small voltage applied to the base pushes current into the emitter, and this current is controlled by, and flows through, the collector once the device enters its active region.

For a BC548 transistor, the base emitter junction needs about 0.7V to turn on and enter the active region. If the base voltage rises too far past this, the transistor enters breakdown and gets damaged. It helps to picture the transistor as two diodes joined together at the base, one at the base emitter junction and one at the base collector junction.

One relationship worth remembering: as Vcc increases, Vbb decreases, but both Ib and Ic increase gradually as a result.

Bipolar Junction Transistor

The BJT is a three terminal device that comes in two types: NPN (negative positive negative) and PNP (positive negative positive). The three terminals are called the base (B), collector (C) and emitter (E).

Highlight of the day

“A diode does not fight the alternating current, it simply refuses to carry it backwards.”

From Notes on Electronics

My note

Physics of Semiconductor Diode Rectification: From Half Wave to Ripple Smoothing

This circuit chain converts alternating current (AC) into stable direct current (DC) by exploiting the one way conduction of a P N junction diode, then smoothing and regulating the output using a capacitor and a Zener diode. Each stage below carries the physics behind it, along with a description of how current flows through the circuit.

1. Half wave rectifier: AC to pulsating DC

An AC source feeds the primary coil of a transformer, where the current alternates direction continuously. The transformer steps this down (or up) across the core to a secondary coil, producing a stepped AC voltage. From the secondary coil, the signal reaches the anode of a diode. Current is only allowed to flow through the diode when the anode is positive relative to the cathode, since this forward biases the junction and produces roughly a 0.7V drop across it for silicon. Once through the diode, current flows downward through the load resistor, but only during the positive half cycle of the input.

A semiconductor diode is a P N junction device that conducts current primarily in one direction. During the positive half cycle of the AC input, the anode becomes positive relative to the cathode, forward biasing the diode. This narrows the depletion region, allowing majority carriers (holes in the P type material, electrons in the N type material) to cross the junction and produce current through the load resistor.

During the negative half cycle, the diode is reverse biased instead: the depletion region widens and forms a high resistance barrier that blocks current. As a result, no voltage appears across the load for that half cycle. The transformer's output voltage follows the turns ratio between the primary and secondary coils, so the final output at this stage is pulsating DC: unidirectional, but not yet constant.

2. Filtering and ripple smoothing: pulsating DC to smoothed DC

The rectified output is fed into a capacitor placed in parallel with the load, which smooths the pulsating DC into something closer to a steady level. During the positive half cycle, while the diode conducts, the capacitor charges rapidly through the diode's low forward resistance, storing energy in its electric field.

When the input voltage falls, either between peaks or during the blocked negative half cycle, the diode becomes reverse biased and stops conducting. At that point the capacitor discharges slowly through the load resistor instead, supplying current and holding the output voltage up until the next charging pulse arrives.

The key figure governing how well this works is the RC time constant, τ = R_L × C, the time it takes the capacitor to discharge to about 37% of its initial voltage. For effective filtering, this time constant must be much larger than the period of the AC ripple: R_L × C ≫ T = 1/f, where f is the ripple frequency (equal to the input AC frequency for a half wave rectifier). When this condition holds, the capacitor's voltage only decays slightly between charging pulses, producing a nearly constant DC level with a small ripple. A larger capacitance gives a longer time constant and therefore a smoother output.

3. Voltage regulation using a Zener diode: smoothed DC to stable DC

From the filter stage, the smoothed DC passes through a switch into a regulation stage built around a Zener diode connected in parallel with the load, downstream of the smoothing capacitor. The Zener diode sits reverse biased in this circuit, and conducts once the voltage across it reaches its rated breakdown voltage, V_Z.

A Zener diode is a specially doped P N junction designed to operate reliably in reverse breakdown at a precise voltage. Once in breakdown, whether through the Zener effect at low voltages or avalanche breakdown at higher voltages, it conducts heavily and holds an almost constant voltage across its terminals, even as the current through it varies. This makes it an effective shunt voltage regulator: if the input voltage rises, the Zener draws more current and drops the excess voltage across the series resistance, keeping the load voltage stable. If the input voltage falls below V_Z, the Zener stops conducting and behaves like an open circuit instead, so regulation is lost at that point.

Linking diodes to transistors

The P N junction is the building block behind both diodes and transistors. A Bipolar Junction Transistor contains two P N junctions arranged back to back, either as NPN or PNP. Its base emitter junction behaves just like a forward biased diode, needing roughly 0.7V for silicon to turn the transistor on.

In power supplies, transistors extend what Zener regulation alone can do: the Zener provides a stable reference voltage, while a transistor amplifies current, letting the overall circuit supply far higher load currents than a Zener diode could manage on its own.

Full power supply chain

Put together, the stages form one continuous chain: an AC input passes through a transformer, which steps the voltage according to its turns ratio (V₂ equals the ratio of secondary to primary turns, multiplied by V₁); the diode rectifier then enforces one way conduction through the P N junction, producing a half wave output; the capacitor filter smooths this using a time constant far greater than the AC period (R_L × C ≫ T); and the Zener stabilizer holds the final output at a stable, regulated voltage, V_Z.

The result is a clean progression from raw alternating current to a steady, regulated DC supply suitable for powering electronic circuits.

Highlight of the day

“A panel's nameplate watts are a promise about a laboratory, not a promise about your roof.”

From Notes on Solar PV

My note

Air Mass and Standard Test Conditions (STC)

More about air mass in solar energy: click here

The air mass coefficient describes how far sunlight actually travels through the atmosphere before reaching a panel, given as a ratio against the shortest possible path: straight up, at the zenith. Outside the atmosphere altogether, air mass is zero, and the irradiance there is about 1,367 W per square metre, known as the solar constant. As the sun's angle moves away from directly overhead, sunlight cuts through more atmosphere, and the air mass ratio rises with it.

An air mass of 1.5, or AM 1.5, means sunlight has passed through one and a half atmosphere thicknesses, corresponding to a solar zenith angle of roughly 48.2°. This value was adopted in the 1970s as the reference spectrum for the industry, chosen because it represents a typical operating sun across the continental United States during prime generating hours, rather than the highest possible sun. The "G" in AM 1.5G stands for global tilted: it accounts for direct beam light, diffuse sky light, and ground reflection together, on a surface tilted 37° toward the equator, which is close to what a fixed tilt module actually sees in practice. This full spectrum is tabulated wavelength by wavelength in ASTM G173 and adopted by the IEC in IEC 60904 3.

The spectrum matters because silicon only responds efficiently to wavelengths from roughly 300 to 1,100 nanometres. A lamp heavy in red and infrared light will flatter a panel compared with one heavy in green and ultraviolet light, so without a standardised spectrum, two labs testing the same panel under different lamps would report different wattages. AM 1.5G is what removes that variable.

The three pinned values of STC

Standard Test Conditions, or STC, is the one fixed laboratory setup that every manufacturer rates a panel against, whether it is a LONGi, a Jinko, or a REC module. It pins down three quantities, and every other electrical spec on a datasheet, Pmax, Vmp, Imp, Voc, Isc, is simply what gets measured once those three are held fixed.

ParameterSTC valueWhat it represents
Plane of array irradiance1,000 W/m²Roughly peak clear sky sunlight, panel facing the sun
Cell temperature25°CThe cell itself, not the surrounding air
Air mass coefficientAM 1.5GSpectrum and path length through 1.5 atmospheres

The 1,000 W/m² figure is a clean round number chosen because ground level irradiance on a clear day, once atmospheric absorption from water vapour, ozone and Rayleigh scattering is accounted for, typically peaks between 1,000 and 1,050 W/m². The 25°C cell temperature is simply room temperature, a convenient and already standard reference point across electrical engineering. Neither figure is meant to describe what a panel experiences in the field.

STC exists purely for fair comparison. The International Electrotechnical Commission formalised it into IEC 61215 in the early 1990s, with IEC 61215 1:2021 as the current edition, precisely so that a lab in Xi'an, a lab in Singapore, and a third party test house in Cologne all arrive at the same wattage for the same panel. Before this existed, manufacturers rated panels however they liked, and comparing two panels meant testing them both under identical outdoor conditions.

How a panel is actually tested

A real panel cannot sit in real 1,000 W/m² sunlight and stay at 25°C, since the cell heats past that within seconds of exposure. Instead, manufacturers use a flash tester: an enclosed cabinet with a xenon arc lamp that fires a single pulse, typically one to ten milliseconds long, calibrated to 1,000 W/m². During that pulse, an electronic load sweeps the panel from short circuit through its maximum power point to open circuit, capturing the full current voltage curve before the cell has time to warm up.

IEC 60904 9 grades flash testers on three criteria: how closely the lamp's spectrum matches AM 1.5G, how evenly the irradiance is spread across the panel, and how stable that irradiance stays during the sweep. Tier one manufacturers run testers graded A+ on all three axes, while lower tier factories sometimes use Class B equipment, which can introduce around 2% uncertainty into the rated wattage.

From that single I-V curve, every electrical spec on the datasheet follows: Pmax is the highest point on the curve, Vmp and Imp are the voltage and current at that point, Voc is where the curve crosses zero current, Isc is where it crosses zero voltage, and fill factor is Pmax divided by Voc times Isc, a measure of how close the curve sits to an ideal rectangle.

Power tolerance and binning

Modern Tier one datasheets quote a tolerance such as 0 / +5 W, meaning a panel sold as 410 W will measure between 410 and 415 W at STC, never below. Panels coming off the flash tester are sorted into wattage bins and labelled accordingly, a practice known as positive tolerance binning that became standard around 2014. Older panels, and some lower tier product still, used a tolerance of plus or minus 3 to 5%, meaning a panel sold as 300 W could genuinely be as low as 285 W. This matters in a string of panels, since the weakest one caps the current for the whole string.

What STC does not tell you

STC is a measurement standard, not a yield forecast. It says nothing about how much energy a panel will produce in a given climate, which depends on irradiance data and the panel's temperature coefficient. It says nothing about degradation, since STC is a day zero figure and a Tier one panel typically retains 87 to 92% of its rated output after 25 years. It also says nothing about cold weather behaviour: on a cold morning, open circuit voltage can run 15 to 20% above the STC figure, which matters when sizing a string against an inverter's maximum input.

Because of the fixed 25°C reference, STC also overstates real world output simply by ignoring heat. A typical rooftop cell runs 20 to 35°C hotter than the surrounding air, so a panel rated at STC can lose 6 to 12% of its nameplate output purely from running hot, before any other loss is counted.

NOCT, temperature coefficient, and reading the I-V curve

NOCT, the Nominal Operating Cell Temperature (also written NMOT), describes the temperature a panel reaches under more realistic conditions: 800 W/m² irradiance, 20°C ambient air, and a light 1 m/s wind, rather than the fixed 25°C cell of STC. Under these conditions a cell typically settles between 42 and 48°C, which makes NOCT a far better indicator of everyday field temperature than the STC figure, even though STC remains the number printed as the headline rating.

The temperature coefficient describes how much a panel's power, voltage or current shifts for every degree the cell moves away from 25°C, usually expressed as a percentage per °C, for example −0.35%/°C for Pmax. Because this figure is negative, a hotter panel produces less power: at 45°C cell temperature with a −0.35%/°C coefficient, a panel loses about 7% of its rated output from heat alone. Two panels with identical STC wattage but different coefficients, say −0.24%/°C for an HJT cell against −0.34%/°C for a PERC cell, can diverge by 5 to 8% on a hot afternoon despite being rated identically on paper.

The I-V curve itself, current plotted against voltage under a given set of conditions, is where all of this is read from. Its two axis crossings are Isc, the short circuit current at zero voltage, and Voc, the open circuit voltage at zero current. Between them sits Pmax, the point on the curve where the product of voltage and current peaks, which is what an MPPT controller in an inverter continuously hunts for as conditions shift through the day.

Fill factor ties the curve's shape to its quality: FF = Pmax ÷ (Voc × Isc), typically between 0.70 and 0.85 for a good panel. A higher fill factor means a squarer, more efficient curve, while a rounded curve with a low fill factor delivers less power than the raw Voc times Isc figure would suggest.

Panel efficiency follows a separate, simpler formula: efficiency equals Pmax divided by the panel's area times 1,000 W/m², expressed as a percentage. Typical commercial panels sit between 18 and 23%. A higher efficiency panel delivers more power from the same roof area, which is a different comparison entirely from a higher wattage panel, since a larger panel can easily out rate a smaller one in raw watts while being no more efficient per square metre.

Put together, the I-V curve is what actually gets measured; Pmax and fill factor are read off that curve; Pmax combined with panel area gives efficiency; the temperature coefficient then adjusts Pmax for whatever the real cell temperature turns out to be; and NOCT is what estimates that real cell temperature in the first place. STC sits underneath all of it as the one fixed reference point that makes every one of these numbers comparable from one datasheet to the next.

Highlight of the day

“An efficient solar energy system is designed as one integrated system, from the solar cell and wiring to the inverter, thermal collector, and energy storage.”

Key discussion: Solar PV and Thermal System Design Integration

What I learned

A comprehensive solar energy system requires careful optimization across electrical and thermal components. The short-circuit current (Isc) and maximum power point tracking (MPPT) work together to maximize energy extraction. String sizing and voltage-drop calculations ensure safe current and voltage levels throughout the DC circuit.

Shading analysis helps determine optimal module placement and predict annual irradiance losses. Bypass diodes protect shaded or damaged cell groups, reducing the risk of hot spots and helping prevent severe localized heating.

PV electrical design

Isc — Short-Circuit Current: The maximum current a solar cell or module produces when its output terminals are shorted together under standard test conditions.

MPPT — Maximum Power Point Tracking: An electronic technique used by an inverter or charge controller to continuously adjust operating voltage and current so the array delivers its maximum possible power.

String sizing: The calculation used to determine how many modules can be connected in series and parallel while remaining within the inverter’s voltage and current limits across expected temperatures.

Voltage-drop calculation: This ensures that resistive conductor losses between the array and inverter remain below a target percentage, typically 1–3%, to protect system efficiency.

Shading analysis: This maps obstructions such as trees, chimneys, and nearby buildings to estimate how much irradiance the array will lose throughout the year.

Module protection

Bypass diodes: Components inside module junction boxes that route current around shaded or damaged cell groups, preventing them from bottlenecking the entire string.

Hot spots: Localized areas of overheating that occur when a mismatched or faulty cell dissipates power instead of generating it. Hot spots can damage the module.

PID — Potential-Induced Degradation: A voltage-driven degradation mechanism in which leakage currents between the cells and module frame cause power loss over time.

Inverter clipping: This occurs when the DC array produces more power than the inverter’s AC rating, causing the excess energy to be curtailed.

DC/AC ratio: Also called the inverter loading ratio, this compares the array’s nameplate DC capacity with the inverter’s AC capacity. It balances energy harvest against clipping losses.

System architectures

String inverters connect modules in series strings that feed a central inverter. They are generally cost-effective but can be more affected by uneven shading.

Microinverters are installed at individual modules, allowing module-level conversion and monitoring. They can perform well on roofs with multiple orientations or partial shading.

Power optimizers operate at module level while feeding a string inverter. They provide module-level optimisation while retaining part of the string-inverter architecture.

Grid connection and safety

Three-phase power delivers electricity through three conductors with a 120° phase offset. It provides smoother, higher-capacity power delivery and is common in commercial systems and larger residential inverters.

Single-phase power uses one alternating-current phase and is common in smaller residential systems. The choice between single-phase and three-phase equipment depends on grid requirements and load characteristics.

Anti-islanding protection

Anti-islanding protection is an inverter safety function that disconnects the solar system from the grid during an outage. This prevents the system from energizing utility lines while workers are repairing them.

Solar thermal collectors

Flat-plate collectors use a dark absorber plate beneath a transparent cover to heat water or air. They are widely used for domestic hot water and other low-temperature applications.

Evacuated tube collectors use vacuum-sealed glass tubes to reduce convective heat loss. They are effective in cold or windy climates.

Concentrating collectors use mirrors or lenses to focus sunlight onto a small receiver, producing high temperatures for industrial process heat or utility-scale thermal power.

Thermal transport and storage

Working fluid is the medium that carries thermal energy from the collector to the point of use or storage. Examples include water, glycol, air, and molten salt.

TES — Thermal Energy Storage stores heat or cold in a medium such as water, phase-change material, or molten salt. The stored thermal energy can be used hours after it was collected, improving load flexibility and system resilience.

Integrated design insight

Together, these electrical and thermal design parameters create an integrated, efficient, and safe renewable energy system. The next step is to model how module temperature, shading, string voltage, inverter limits, collector efficiency, working-fluid flow, and thermal storage affect the system’s expected performance.
